The Sound of Spotify, 2015–2025

Authors: Jiho Lee, John Li, Ed Yun, Andrew Zhang

Over the last decade, the hits in your feed didn’t all move in one direction—some years got brighter, some heavier, some easier to dance to, and not always at the same time. The surprise in the numbers is often quieter: there isn’t one dial you can turn to “explain” popularity; people keep liking different kinds of songs, and the averages are just where the crowd leans for a moment. Pick a slice in Step 2, and every chart below stays on that same pile of tracks while you poke at the easy stories.

The tension

Everyone wants a tidy headline about how hits “moved” across 2015–2025—until the years or the scene changes, and the picture tilts. One static chart can’t settle whose story is fair, and it can’t show you how thin the “rules” really are when people keep liking different kinds of songs at the same time.

What we built to show that

Pick years and a segment once; every view below—including the little myth charts and the closing recap—runs on that exact subset. We’re not fishing for a single predictor; we’re stress-testing the tidy headlines (faster, happier, shorter) on the same songs. When the lines and dots refuse to line up, that is the finding—popular music stays a wide cloud, and filters only change which crowd you’re describing.

The thread we’re pulling

Spotify tags each track with rough “vibes in numbers”—mood, energy, how danceable it feels, tempo, length—and a popularity score. None of that replaces listening, and none of it hands you a formula: mostly we see overlapping tastes, modest averages, and a lot of scatter. What we can do is watch how that cloud drifts (or doesn’t) across 2015–2025, then change the window and ask whether the same campfire story still sounds true.

Skim the opening curve, then tighten the years or pick a segment. The middle of the page is where your slice takes shape; the small charts at the end poke at the easy explanations people repeat at parties; the last panel is a plain-English recap—not a verdict on what “works,” but a snapshot of how messy the hits still are under one lens.

A tiny decoder ring

Most lines use 0–1 scores (higher = more of that quality). Popularity is 0–100 on Spotify’s own scale; tempo is BPM.

Valence — “How sunny does it sound?” (rough mood; not lyrics).

Energy — Loud, busy, forward motion—not the same as happy.

Danceability — Steady beat, easy to move to.

Popularity — Spotify’s blend of recent and long-run listening; not raw streams.

Step 5b · Pressure test the profile

Which simple explanations hold up—and when do they fall apart?

Three campfire arguments about pop—did hits get happier? do smashes cluster in one corner of dance vs energy? are songs shrinking?—each gets a tiny chart and a cheeky yellow verdict for whatever slice you’re holding. Read them as provocations, not courtroom evidence.

If a pattern survives every scene you throw at it, maybe it’s a real decade-wide habit. If it vanishes the second you switch segments, that’s the same lesson from another angle: listening isn’t one taste, and “the data” only ever describes the crowd you turned the knob toward.
